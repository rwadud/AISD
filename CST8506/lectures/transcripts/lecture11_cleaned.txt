Why? Since we are combining ten different classifiers and I take the majority vote to produce a decision, the question is: is that correct or not? It's about explaining the decision.

The majority voting, bagging, or ensemble approach takes the output of different experts, different classifiers, and simply combines the results. So when somebody asks you why you made this decision, if you say, "OK, I received these decisions and I'm combining them," that is not an explanation. The end user is looking for the exact explanation: why the first classifier made that decision, why the second classifier made the other decision, why the third classifier made the other decision. The end user is not looking for the explanation from ten different classifiers. They want a short and useful explanation.

This was a very simple example with only one feature. If we have data with hundreds of features, or with millions of features, you cannot even explain a single classifier. That is why we say the ensemble methods are weak in explanation. The ensemble is too long and too complex to explain why C_i predicted a particular label. We are looking for transparency. If you say, "we take the majority voting on the outputs of several classifiers," that is not transparent, because you don't know anything about why C1, C2, C4, C5 predicted those decisions.

On the other side, we have the boosting method. In bagging, we simply sub-sample the training data to create different training sets, with replacement, without considering the importance of the samples. We treat all the samples equally. But it's not perfect. Most of the time, some samples are difficult to predict, and some samples are very easy to get the correct answer for. That is normal.

You are studying machine learning, advanced machine learning, NLP, or reinforcement learning. Just because you are studying these courses doesn't mean all of you have the same ability in all the different topics you have studied. For example, you might be good at SVM, or good at NLP, or good at some other topic. Different classifiers can have different knowledge on different regions.

If you know that you are not good at Q-learning from the beginning, what would be the solution? What are you going to do? Instead of reviewing the definition of reinforcement learning, where we have different components (environment, agent, reward, and so forth), you should focus on your weak areas. If you studied the same stuff 100 times, and it is not relevant to Q-learning, your Q-learning would not be improved.

We have the same thing here. We train a classifier with some sample of the training data, then apply that classifier on the full original training data, not on the test set. That's the important point: we test it on the training data. We look at the results, and we look at the samples that our classifier missed.

Let me map this to our reinforcement learning study scenario. I am studying reinforcement learning, and I have time before the final exam. Today I started reviewing. I planned to review all of the material ten times. At the first step, I think I am good in all the topics, so I review everything. Then I try to solve some sample questions. I realize that I don't know Q-learning, I don't know deep Q-learning, I don't know SARSA, I don't know TD. But I know the definition of reinforcement learning. So in the next review, what are we going to do? If your brain is already good on the definition parts, there is no need to review them. You put more focus on your weak points: TD, SARSA, Q-learning. Then you test yourself again with all the topics. If you realize that your Q-learning improved, you can take a day off on it. There is no need to review it again, because your brain can degrade something if you focus on it too much. Then you continue to improve your ability on all the topics.

This is an iterative procedure to adapt to the changing distribution of training data. We change the distribution of training. At the first step, we treat every topic equally. In the second review, we change the subject: we forget about the simple definitions and put more focus on TD, SARSA, and the others. In the next step, again, the distribution is changed.

What is the difference between this and bagging? Weights. With bagging, in all the review steps, you take the topics at random. You choose the topics at random. So it would be possible, even if you are weak in Q-learning, to review ten times without ever touching Q-learning.

Initially, all N records, our original samples, are assigned equal weights. There is no difference between the topics. Unlike bagging, weights may change at the end of each boosting round. The weights of the samples can be changed. Records that are misclassified, the topics we realize we are weak on, will have their weights increased. If we miss Q-learning, its weight must be increased. If we remember the definition of reinforcement learning, its weight would be decreased.

For example, here we have three rounds. At the first round, everything is taken equally. We just do random sampling with replacement. Then in the second round, you see the probability of sample 4 appear three times. Why? Because most probably, at the first round, the classifier misclassified sample 4. Then the second classifier also misclassified sample 4. That is why you see 4, 5, 5, 4 in the round-two sample.

Is it clear? The weight of the point: in the previous classifier, we missed 4, so its weight increased, and by increasing the weight, the probability of selecting 4 is increased. In round 3, the model can correctly classify 4, so the weight of 4 will be decreased. The probability of selecting 5 would be increased, and for the others it would be decreased.

From the implementation perspective, how do we actually draw samples with these weights? Let's say we have samples 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 inside a bag. When I say that at the first step the probability of being selected is equal for all samples, it means when I put my hand inside the bag and take one of them, the probability is the same for each. For the next sample, I return it to the bag and do the same. But in boosting, after the first step, the probability of these samples changes.

Let me explain how to interpret the process. I put my hand into the bag. I want to consider the probability as the selection weight. How do I reflect a higher probability? How do I incorporate weights into my selection? The simplest way is to duplicate entries. For example, if one sample should be drawn at 25% and another at 35%, I duplicate the entries accordingly. Yes, we need to assign a weight. Assigning the weight is important; the implementation question is how we use those weights during selection.

In bagging, we don't consider any importance for any sample, and we don't take into account any importance weight for the classifiers either. But in AdaBoost, or AdaBoost.M1, we consider the weight both for the samples to be selected and for the classifiers.

So we have base classifiers. For each classifier, we find the error rate. What is w_j^i, and what is N? N is the number of samples in our original training set. w_j^i is the weight for sample j under classifier i, multiplied by an indicator that is 0 or 1: if classifier i predicts the label for x_j correctly, meaning C_i(x_j) equals y_j, the indicator is 0; otherwise it is 1. The error rate is the weighted average of these indicators across samples.

Based on the error rate, we define the importance weight for this classifier: alpha_i equals one half times the natural logarithm of (1 minus error_i) divided by error_i. This is the weight for the classifier. The error rate determines it.

Then we need to update the weight of the sample. The weight of sample j in the next run becomes the current weight of sample j, divided by the normalization factor Z_i (the sum of all the current weights), times e to the power of minus alpha_i if classifier i classifies sample j correctly. That means w_j at the next round would be decreased. Otherwise, if classifier i misclassifies x_j, the coefficient is e to the power of alpha_i, so the importance of sample j for the next round would be increased.

There is also a safeguard. If any intermediate round produces an error rate higher than 50%, it means the classifier is worse than random guessing. At that step we will not update the weights. Instead, we reset the weights to 1/N and go back to resampling.

Now let's talk about weighted majority voting. In simple majority voting, I treat all the classifiers equally. But in weighted majority voting, I consider the importance of each classifier. If I know that one of the experts was the best student ever, graduated from the best university in the world, and had lots of scientific papers, while the others did not have such credentials, it means I should put more weight on that expert. This is the way it usually is in life. You ask a question of different people, but you give more weight to the one you are sure is experienced in that area. We do the same thing here: we consider the classifier's rate in our majority-voting process.

Now moving to association rule mining. The arrangement of items in a store, the way they are placed in different locations, is one kind of information we can study. Let me introduce the main concepts.

We have an itemset, a collection of one or more items. For example, this is one transaction. Bread and milk is one itemset. We have the k-itemset concept: an itemset that contains k items. For example, a 3-itemset.

Then we have the support count: the frequency of occurrence of an itemset. For example, the support count for bread and milk is how many times we see bread and milk appear together. For bread and milk: one, two, three. For milk, bread, and diaper: one, two. The count is two.

The support is the fraction of transactions that contain an itemset. In our example, for milk, bread, and diaper, it is two out of five transactions.

Then we have the concept of frequent itemset. We use the support count, the support, and this frequent-itemset concept to find association rules. A frequent itemset is an itemset whose support is greater than or equal to the minsup threshold, the minimum support.

An association rule is an implication expression of the form X to Y, where X and Y are itemsets. For example, {Milk, Diaper} to {Beer}. How do we evaluate our rules? Based on support and confidence. For this rule, the support is how many times we can see both X and Y together, the union of X and Y. Confidence measures how often items in Y appear in transactions that contain X. Based on the confidence and support scores, and on thresholds we set for each, we can evaluate the rule. If a rule is valid, we trust it, and we can do something based on it.

These are the reasons I said this is not very difficult. Just by looking at this set of transactions, you see we have a very small number of items. In total, we have six items. We can simply list different kinds of rules like these, compute the support and confidence, check them against our thresholds, and decide about their validity.

But if we have D unique items, the total number of itemsets is 2 to the power of D. The total number of possible association rules is 3 to the power of D, minus 2 to the power of (D plus 1), plus 1. Even if we only have 6 items, the number of possible association rules is 602. Now imagine how many items we have in practice. You cannot do any kind of brute-force or exhaustive search, enumerating all possible rules, checking confidence and support, and making decisions based on those values. This is where the Apriori association rule mining algorithm comes in.

For the next two lectures, you have to cover hyperparameter tuning. I believe you all know what hyperparameter tuning is. Is there anyone who doesn't? Hyperparameter tuning is finding the best possible values for the hyperparameters of any algorithm. We need to set them before training. The difference between hyperparameters and parameters is that hyperparameters we give the value for, but parameters are learned during training. Different algorithms have different parameters and different hyperparameters. For example, for K-means, the number of clusters is a hyperparameter, and the final cluster centroids (the cluster representatives) are learned parameters.

Why is this important? Finding good values for the hyperparameters ensures optimal model performance.

There are four approaches. Manual search: just try everything one by one. Grid search: first, define the set of possible values for each hyperparameter, then train your model with every combination of those values. Random search: again it is similar, but instead of trying all combinations, you just choose random combinations from the predefined values.

The fourth approach, which is usually used in scientific or academic work, is to refer to the literature, or to use best practices. Most of the time when you publish a paper, the reviewers ask you why you chose these values for these hyperparameters, why you didn't use other values. In that case, you say these values are the popular values among other researchers. For example, for batch size, you can use any value, but usually we use 32 or 64, because most people use 32 or 64.
