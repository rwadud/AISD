When we have an imbalanced class problem, or for credit card fraud, we evaluate based on the confusion matrix rather than on accuracy, because most samples here belong to the negative class.

So what are the alternative measures? Precision. What kind of information does precision show? Precision is a over a plus c. Here, a plus c is the number of samples predicted as positive, and a is the number of correctly predicted positives. So a over a plus c tells us, out of all samples predicted as positive, how many were actually positive.

Similarly, recall is a over a plus b, where a plus b is the total number of actual positive samples. So recall asks: among all the real positive samples, how many were predicted correctly.

Then why do we need another measure like the F-score when we already have two well-defined metrics? The answer is simple. If you compare two models with only one metric, comparison is easy: based on the value of that metric, one is better than the other. But once the number of metrics grows, comparison becomes difficult. For example, suppose you have two models where one has high recall and low precision, and the other has low recall and high precision. How can we compare them? To solve this, we define another metric like the F-measure; specifically, this one is the F1 score.

In general, we have the F-beta score. Beta represents the importance of recall with respect to precision; it expresses how many times recall is more important than precision. When we say F1 score, beta equals 1, so we treat their importance as equal. But most of the time their importance is not equal.

For example, in disease detection, both are important. For COVID detection, which is more important? Recall, because you need to find most of the people who are actually infected by COVID. Precision is also important, but just a little.

So in this confusion matrix, precision is 10 over 10 plus 10. If you now have two models like these, the first has an accuracy of 99%, and for the second the accuracy looks similar but the underlying precision, recall, and F-measure differ. Just by looking at accuracy, you cannot tell which model handles the minority class better.

For both models, we have additional metrics. The true negative rate is TN divided by TN plus FP; this sum represents all the real negative samples, and the ratio shows, out of all negative samples, how many were correctly classified. The false positive rate, sometimes denoted alpha, is FP divided by TN plus FP, which equals 1 minus specificity, and it tells us, out of all negative samples, how many were classified as positive. Then we have the FN rate, FN divided by FN plus TP (the total number of real positive samples); it tells us how many real positive samples were predicted as negative.

These metrics are for classification tasks. For different machine learning tasks we have different evaluations. For machine translation we have other measurements like BLEU; for text generation we have another one such as perplexity. We can also compare models based on precision-recall curves.

If you have an F-measure of 80% and accuracy of 80%, the model looks strong. If F-measure is very low while accuracy is similar, performance is poor on the minority class, and we judge the model based on F-measure, which is more significant here. You will also hear about different versions of these metrics such as micro score, macro score, and weighted scores, but not all are used everywhere; you should know which metric fits each situation.

In college or university we train models for grades, but in reality we should train models for use. Not every model we train should be deployed; there must be some criteria for that decision.

So you train a model and obtain these results. The last one is better compared to the previous two models, but the question is still: are you going to deploy it? If the model is worse than a random guess (random gives 50%, and this one is only 28%), forget about it. If it is better than random, you then decide based on the problem. As I mentioned, for some problems recall is much more important than precision, and in those cases we prefer the model with higher recall.

By looking at the difference between precision and recall, you can also tell whether your training dataset was balanced or imbalanced. With an imbalanced dataset, the difference between precision and recall is usually large, while with a balanced dataset they are similar. For example, if you have 90 positive samples but only 10 are predicted correctly, recall on the positive class is very low.

Now, the ROC, or Receiver Operating Characteristic. This is a graphical approach showing the trade-off between detection rate and false alarm rate, and we use it to display model performance and compare different models. It was developed in the 1950s for signal detection. The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR.

At (0,0) on the TPR-FPR axes, we declare everything to be the negative class, so the true positive rate is zero and the false positive count is also zero. At (1,1), we declare everything positive: all positive samples are predicted as positive, but all negatives are also predicted as positive.

To plot an ROC curve across thresholds, we need continuous-valued output from the classifier. For decision trees, at a leaf we have a set of positive and negative samples. Instead of assigning a discrete label, we take the fraction of positive samples over all samples at that leaf. This converts the output into a ranking from the most likely positive instance to the least likely, and by using different thresholds on this continuous value, we can create different variations of the classifier with different TPR and FPR.

Many classifiers produce only discrete values, so we convert them by dividing the number of positive samples by all the samples in the node. Then, based on different threshold values, we make decisions on class labels. For example, any sample with a value higher than the threshold is labeled positive. Each threshold gives a different confusion matrix.

Given a one-dimensional feature with two classes, we have only one variable, and any sample located at x greater than t (the threshold, also called alpha) is classified as positive. If we place t somewhere, all samples greater than t on one side are labeled positive, and the others negative. If we change t, everything changes. Sweeping t across all values, we get true positive rates and false positive rates, plotted based on the threshold we use.

For the ROC curve, we have scores and true classes for each sample, and we follow these steps. Use a classifier that produces a continuous score for each instance; the higher the score, the more likely the instance is positive. Sort the instances in decreasing order by score. Apply a threshold at each unique value of the score, and for each threshold compute the true positive and false positive counts to obtain TPR and FPR.

Consider the sample list. First we take t equal to 0.25. All samples with score below 0.25 are predicted as negative, and all with score greater than or equal to 0.25 are predicted as positive. With threshold 0.25, every sample's score meets the threshold, so every prediction is positive. How many of these are correct positives? Five. So true positives equal five, and the remaining five are false positives. Since no sample is predicted as negative, true negatives and false negatives are zero.

Next, take t equal to 0.43. All samples with score below 0.43 are predicted as negative, and all greater than or equal to 0.43 are predicted as positive. Among those positive predictions, how many were truly positive? Four. So true positives are four. We count false positives, true negatives, and false negatives similarly.

Essentially, the classifier is better than random for small threshold values. In general, if you have two classifiers with two ROC curves and you want to compare them, look at the area under the curve; the classifier with the maximum AUC has the better ROC. This way we can compare classifiers just by looking at the ROC curve.

For example, suppose we have two models M1 and M2, with the diagonal representing random guessing. In this region M1 is better for small FPR: the area under M1 is much higher than that under M2. But for large FPR, M2 is better. So if most of the area is before FPR equal to 0.5, the curve for M2 is lower in that region.

What does lower FPR and higher TPR mean? After training, you should analyze your results: where your model works fine, where it does not, and on which kinds of samples. If the FPR for a model is high, it means the model predicts most negative samples as positive. When FPR is low, the model correctly classifies negative samples. So if you have different models, when your false positive rate is low you can use M1, and when it is higher you can use M2.

That covers evaluation. What about solutions to handle the imbalance problem before training? The solution is simple: we need to balance the dataset by sampling. Sampling can be done two ways, undersampling or oversampling. Undersampling means removing samples from the majority class, while oversampling means increasing samples in the minority class. Based on how we apply undersampling or oversampling, different sampling algorithms have been proposed; if you search, you will find many. At its simplest, we have random undersampling and random oversampling. We also have SMOTE, a specific oversampling technique that artificially creates new samples.

For undersampling, assume we have 9,000 majority samples. We reduce the majority class by randomly removing some samples. That is random undersampling.

For oversampling with 9,000 majority samples, we want to increase the minority class. We simply sample randomly with replacement, which means after random oversampling we have many duplicates of our data. Neither oversampling nor undersampling is guaranteed to solve the imbalance problem.

Then we have SMOTE, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique. The logic behind SMOTE is simple. Random oversampling replicates data, which just duplicates it. With SMOTE we oversample without replicating the exact same data; we generate new data. For each minority class instance, we take a nearest neighbor and add a new sample along the line segment between the instance and the neighbor.

Question: SMOTE works because all samples are numeric. If we are working in text classification where samples are text and we also have a class imbalance problem, how do we handle it? One student suggested translating the text into a vector representation, so we have a vector and can apply the method normally. The issue is that when we convert text to a numeric representation, interpolating these vectors may not map back to a valid token. So instead of a direct application, you can use text perturbation, like replacing one word with its synonym. Another simple way: with today's text generation ability, we can give a piece of text and generate thousands of similar texts.

Now let's move on to outliers. What are anomalies or outliers? They are a set of data points considered different from the rest. What is the difference between noise and an outlier? Noise is not a valid data point, while an outlier is a valid data point but different from the norm of the data. For example, say the average height of people is 1.80 meters. If you find someone 3 meters tall, the value is valid, but it is different from the norm. Usually we want to find these outliers because they affect the mean, since most people are not 3 meters tall. Outliers may occur one in a thousand, and context is important: for example, freezing temperatures in July matter in context. An outlier can be important or a nuisance. A very high blood pressure is important and we need to detect it. But, for example, 200 pounds for a two-year-old might be too much.

Anomalies or outliers come from data of different classes, natural variation, or data errors. There are different approaches to outlier detection. Statistically, an outlier is an object that has a low probability with respect to the probability distribution model of the data, so if you find the probability distribution for the data, the data points with the lowest probability are most likely outliers. Methods like Grubbs' test can be used to check whether a dataset contains outliers.

We also have proximity-based or distance-based approaches: anomalies are points far from other points, and the outlier score of an object is the distance to its kth nearest neighbor. We give this outlier score to each sample and judge whether the sample is an outlier. For the density-based approach, we have density scoring. We also have clustering-based rules: points far from cluster centers are outliers. First we have to find the cluster centers, and small clusters can also be considered outliers. We have another technique, reconstruction-based methods, which usually use autoencoders. We lower the dimension of the data using an autoencoder, then reverse the mapping; we look at the difference between the reconstructed data and the original, and if the difference is high the point is an outlier, otherwise it is normal. We also have the one-class SVM model: we apply one-class SVM on our unlabeled data to find the boundary of the normal data, and all samples outside this boundary are labeled as outliers.

For the density-based outlier score in terms of k nearest neighbors, one definition is the inverse of the distance to the kth neighbor, where the inverse of distance defines density. Another definition is the inverse of the average distance to the k neighbors, one over the average of those distances. A point is an outlier not just because it is far from others, but because its density is much less than that of its neighbors. If there are regions of different density, this approach can have problems, since it works based on the region's density. If our dataset has some regions with low density, this simple method struggles.

For that case, we use LOF, the Local Outlier Factor. To apply LOF, we consider the density of a point relative to its k nearest neighbors, y1 through yk. The relative density is the average density of the k nearest neighbors divided by the density of the point. If this number is much greater than 1, x is considered an outlier.

For clustering-based outliers, an object is an outlier if it is not close enough to a prototype-based cluster center, or if its density is too low for a density-based cluster. For reconstruction error, we bring the object back to the original space and call it x-hat; the reconstruction error is the difference between the original object and x-hat, measured in the original space. If this error is low, the object is not an outlier; if it is high, the object is an outlier. How do we compute this? Using a dimensionality reduction model like PCA, or whatever dimensionality reduction methods you have. Another way is to apply an autoencoder: the output tries to reproduce the input, and the hidden layer serves as a dimensionality reduction technique. Autoencoders are also used for noise reduction.
