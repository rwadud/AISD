We started by reviewing the class imbalance problem. In many classification tasks, the classes are highly skewed, meaning one class has far more samples than the other. Typical examples include credit card fraud detection, intrusion detection, defective products in a manufacturing line, and disease detection.

The key point is that accuracy is not a reliable evaluation measure for imbalanced data. Suppose a dataset has 1,000 samples, with only 10 positive cases and 990 negative cases. If a model predicts every sample as negative, its accuracy is 99%, but the model is useless because it detects none of the minority-class cases. In these problems, the correct detection of the minority class is usually much more important than the correct detection of the majority class.

Because of that, for imbalanced classification we focus on measures such as precision, recall, and F-score rather than accuracy alone. Precision tells us, among all samples predicted as positive, how many were actually positive. Recall tells us, among all actually positive samples, how many were predicted correctly. The F-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

We also reviewed how to interpret the confusion-matrix-based metrics. Precision is true positives divided by true positives plus false positives. Recall is true positives divided by true positives plus false negatives. Precision is also called positive predictive value. Recall is also called sensitivity or true positive rate. Specificity is the true negative rate. The false positive rate is one minus specificity, and the false negative rate is one minus sensitivity.

These measures are not limited to classification. They are also important in information retrieval. For example, when you search in Google, sometimes precision matters more, and sometimes recall matters more. It depends on the application and what kind of error is more costly.

In the ideal case, precision and recall should be close to each other. But with class imbalance, there is usually a gap between them. Most of the time, in imbalanced-class settings, precision is higher than recall.

We then reviewed ROC analysis. ROC stands for Receiver Operating Characteristic. It shows the relationship between the true positive rate and the false positive rate. To compare models, we often look at the area under the ROC curve, or AUC. In general, a model with a larger area under the curve is better than a model with a smaller area.

I also mentioned that the model with the best overall AUC is not always the best model for every kind of sample. The procedure is: first cluster the training samples, then apply each candidate model on a representative subset of each cluster to see which model performs best on which cluster. At prediction time, a new sample is first assigned to one of the clusters, and then the model that was previously chosen as best for that cluster is used for the prediction. That approach is more complicated, but it shows that different models may be better for different parts of the data.

To deal with class imbalance, the simplest solution is resampling. There are two basic directions. One is undersampling, where we reduce the number of majority-class samples. The other is oversampling, where we increase the number of minority-class samples. The simplest versions are random undersampling and random oversampling.

For multiclass problems, deciding which class to oversample or undersample is usually a hyperparameter choice. You may pick one class as a reference and adjust the others relative to it, or choose target class sizes heuristically and then evaluate whether the resulting model performs better.

We also reviewed SMOTE, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique. For each minority-class sample, we find its k nearest neighbors from the minority class, not from the majority class. Then we take the difference between the sample and one of its nearest neighbors, multiply that difference by a random number between 0 and 1, and add it back to the original sample. This creates a new synthetic sample along the line segment between the two minority samples. We repeat the process until we have created enough synthetic data. This works naturally for numeric data.

The interpolation logic of SMOTE assumes a vector-space representation, so it fits ordinary numeric feature data directly. For raw images or other highly structured objects, applying SMOTE naively in pixel space is usually not meaningful unless the data have first been embedded into a representation where interpolation is sensible.

After that, we moved to anomaly detection. Anomalies or outliers are samples that are considerably different from the normal data. They are usually rare. An outlier is different from noise. Noise refers to invalid or corrupted data, while an outlier can be valid but unusual.

For example, if employee salaries are usually between 100K and 120K, a salary of 900K might be an outlier. It may still be valid, but it is unusual. On the other hand, if the birth date is invalid, the currency is wrong, or a value was entered in the wrong format, that is noise or a data-entry error. Noise is invalid, but outliers can still be valid samples.

Outliers can damage data analysis if we are trying to understand the general pattern of a dataset. Because of that, we often want to detect them and, depending on the goal, exclude them from the analysis. Some anomalies come from different classes being mixed together. For example, if we are measuring the weight of oranges but a few grapefruits are mixed in, those samples may appear anomalous. Some anomalies come from natural variation, such as unusually tall people. Context matters. In a basketball-player dataset, a very tall person is not unusual, but in another dataset it might be.

We reviewed several anomaly-detection approaches: statistical methods, proximity-based methods, density-based methods, clustering-based methods, reconstruction-based methods, and one-class SVM. In that discussion, similarity was treated as one way of expressing proximity.

For reconstruction-based methods, we discussed the autoencoder. An autoencoder can use different neural-network architectures, such as an MLP or CNN. The idea is to give the model an input and force it to reconstruct the same input at the output. The encoder compresses the data into a lower-dimensional hidden representation, and the decoder reconstructs the original sample from that representation.

One use of the autoencoder is data compression. If the original input is high-dimensional and the hidden representation is much smaller, then instead of storing the full original sample, we can store the encoded representation and later decode it when needed.

Another use is noise reduction. If we have noisy data and we know what the clean version should look like, we can give the noisy sample as input and the clean sample as the target output. That forces the model to learn to remove noise from the input.

The same reconstruction idea can be used for anomaly detection. If the input is normal, the reconstructed output should be very close to the input. If the input is contaminated or anomalous, the reconstruction error should be larger. In that sense, the difference between the input and the output acts as an indication of contamination.

We then discussed one-class SVM. In ordinary classification, we need labeled data from multiple classes, but in anomaly detection we often do not have labels. With one-class SVM, we try to learn a boundary around the normal data. Samples outside that boundary, or sufficiently far from it, are treated as outliers.

The geometric idea is the origin trick. In feature space, we treat the origin as the opposing reference and learn a hyperplane that separates the normal data from the origin with as large a margin as possible, while still allowing some slack for outliers. With a Gaussian kernel, the data are mapped into a high-dimensional space where the model can carve out a flexible decision region around the dense normal samples. The decision function can be written as f of x equals w dot phi of x minus rho. If f of x is greater than or equal to zero, the sample is treated as normal. If f of x is less than zero, it is treated as an outlier. The hyperparameter nu controls the expected fraction of outliers and therefore how tight or loose the learned normal region becomes.

After that, we moved to classifier fusion, or ensemble learning. The main idea is simple: instead of relying on one classifier, we construct several classifiers and combine their predictions to produce a final result. It is like asking for the opinions of several experts instead of relying on just one expert.

For ensemble learning to work well, the classifiers in the pool should be diverse. They should not all behave identically. We can create diversity along two axes — the training data and the classifier — and enumerating the combinations gives five concrete approaches: (1) different training sets with the same learning algorithm; (2) the same training set with the same classifier but different hyperparameter settings; (3) different training sets with the same classifier and different hyperparameter settings; (4) the same training set with different types of classifiers, such as SVM, naive Bayes, and neural networks; (5) different training sets with different classifiers. The most general case combines all of these: a single pool can mix many copies of SVMs with different settings, many neural networks with different architectures, several decision trees, and so on, all trained on different bootstrap samples.

We also noted that classifier diversity itself can be measured, so there are metrics that try to quantify how different the classifiers in a pool really are.

The simplest way to combine classifiers is majority voting, but that is not always a good choice because it treats all classifiers as equally reliable. If one classifier performs much better than another, it should have more influence. That is why weighted majority voting is often better than simple majority voting.

Before combining classifiers, we can also apply classifier selection. In other words, instead of combining all classifiers in the pool, we first choose a promising subset and combine only those. In general, the ensemble-learning pipeline can be seen as classifier generation, classifier selection, and classifier combination.

One simple classifier-selection method is forward selection. First, sort the classifiers by performance. Then start with the best classifier and keep adding one classifier at a time. After each addition, combine the current set of classifiers, evaluate the result on validation data, and keep the new classifier only if it improves the overall performance.

Another method is backward elimination. Start with the full set of classifiers, combine all of them, and evaluate the ensemble. Then remove the lowest-performing classifier and evaluate again. If the result improves, keep that classifier removed and continue. Otherwise, keep it and move to the next candidate.

These are the same strategies used for feature selection, with one important difference. In feature selection there is no majority vote. For forward selection over features, you add one feature at a time, train a model on the current feature subset, evaluate on validation data, and keep the feature only if the score improves. For backward elimination over features, you start with all features, train and evaluate, then remove one feature at a time and check whether the score improves. Features usually cannot be sorted by individual performance the way classifiers can, so backward elimination over features typically removes in a fixed order rather than strictly worst-first.

You can also do classifier selection manually, by inspecting not just aggregate performance but which samples each classifier gets right or wrong. Two classifiers with similar accuracy can still be very different if they succeed and fail on different samples, and that difference is exactly what we mean by diversity. The mapping between feature selection and classifier selection is useful to remember: correlation between features in feature selection corresponds to diversity between classifiers in classifier selection. Low feature correlation is good, and low classifier correlation — which is high diversity — is also good, for the same underlying reason.

We can also use random selection, heuristics, or metaheuristic optimization methods to choose the subset of classifiers. When the pool is large, this becomes a hard combinatorial problem. For example, if you have 500 classifiers, the number of possible non-empty subsets is two to the power of 500 minus 1. That is why methods such as genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, simulated annealing, and other metaheuristics can be useful.

When we combine classifiers, we do it sample by sample by aggregating the predicted labels for the same input sample.

We reviewed why ensemble methods tend to work. They usually work best with unstable base classifiers, meaning classifiers that are sensitive to small changes in the training data or hyperparameters. Examples include decision trees and neural networks. These models often have low bias but high variance, so combining them can reduce variance.

That led us to bias and variance. Overfitting corresponds to low bias and high variance. Underfitting corresponds to high bias and low variance. In practice, overfitting usually comes from excessive model complexity, small datasets, or training that allows the model to memorize details of the data. Another cause of overfitting is data leakage: if a feature in the training data effectively reveals the label, the model will rely on that shortcut instead of learning the real pattern, and performance will collapse on any data where the leaking feature is not present. Underfitting can come from a model that is too simple, insufficient training, poor data quality, or not enough useful signal in the features. As a rough rule, model complexity is often reflected in the architecture and in the number of parameters.

To illustrate model capacity, I also used text processing as an example. The same text can support tasks such as sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, part-of-speech tagging, summarization, topic modeling, and dependency analysis. We also noted that one model can sometimes be trained for multiple tasks at once, which is the idea of multi-task learning.

Increasing the amount of data can help with both underfitting and overfitting, but the reason is not obvious and it is worth spelling out. If you have a small dataset and you train your model for many epochs, the model will memorize those samples and overfit. If you have the same small dataset but you train for only a few epochs, the model will not have time to memorize and will instead underfit. In both cases, increasing the amount of training data helps: with more data, memorization becomes harder, so overfitting is less likely even with many epochs, and there is more signal to learn from in the same number of epochs, so underfitting is less likely even with few epochs. That is why "get more data" is a reasonable answer to both problems, even though overfitting and underfitting are opposite failure modes. Regularization methods such as weight penalties or dropout work differently — they reduce the effective complexity of the model rather than adding information. In general, model complexity is a central factor in overfitting, and dataset size, model complexity, and training length interact together.

We then reviewed bagging. Bagging stands for bootstrap aggregating. We create multiple bootstrap samples by sampling the training data with replacement. A bootstrap sample can contain duplicates, and some original samples may be missing from a particular bootstrap sample. We train one classifier on each bootstrap sample and then combine their predictions, usually by majority voting.

The intuition is that even if we use the same learning algorithm, different training samples produce different classifiers. That creates diversity. Bagging is not tied to a single classifier family; in principle, the base learner can be an SVM, a decision tree, a neural model, or another classifier. We walked through a one-dimensional example with decision stumps, where each stump classified data using a threshold on x. Different bootstrap samples led to different split points, such as 0.35, 0.7, or 0.75. Then, for a new sample, we aggregated the predictions of all the stumps and used the sign of the sum, or equivalently majority voting, as the final prediction.

A practical weakness of ensemble methods is explainability. For a single decision stump, it is easy to say why it predicted a label: for example, because x was less than 0.35. But for an ensemble, saying that several classifiers voted and the majority won is not a useful explanation for an end user. The final decision may be better, but the reasoning is less transparent.

After bagging, we moved to boosting. The key idea of boosting is that not all samples are equally difficult. Instead of treating every sample the same in every round, boosting focuses more on the samples that previous classifiers handled incorrectly. We used a study analogy: if you already understand some topics well, you should spend more time on the weak areas rather than reviewing everything uniformly.

In boosting, all samples start with equal weights. After training a classifier, we apply it to the original training set and identify the samples it misclassified. The weights of misclassified samples are increased, and the weights of correctly classified samples are decreased. In the next round, the training data are sampled with replacement according to these updated weights, so hard samples are more likely to appear again.

We then discussed AdaBoost. For each round, we create a weighted bootstrap sample, train a base classifier, apply it to the original training set, compute its weighted error rate, and then assign that classifier an importance weight alpha based on its error. The sample weights are updated using the classifier's importance. If a sample is classified correctly, its weight is reduced. If it is classified incorrectly, its weight is increased. If a classifier performs worse than random guessing, its error is too high to trust for reweighting, so the weights are reset and the resampling step is repeated. At the end, the final decision is made by weighted majority voting, where each classifier contributes according to its alpha value.

Compared with bagging, boosting is usually not as easy to parallelize because each round depends on the updated weights from the previous round. Bagging is easier to parallelize because the bootstrap samples and classifiers can be trained independently. Boosting often performs well because it focuses on hard samples, but it is not guaranteed to outperform bagging in every problem.

We also mentioned random forests. A random forest is similar to bagging, but the base classifiers must be decision trees. In addition to bootstrap sampling of the training data, the algorithm also randomizes the features by selecting a subset of features at each split. This decorrelates the trees and reduces variance. We also briefly mentioned gradient boosting as another popular boosting family.

Then we moved to association rule mining. This is more from data mining than from machine learning, but it is still useful. The goal is: given a set of transactions, find rules that describe which items tend to occur together. A common example is market-basket analysis.

We reviewed the basic definitions. An itemset is a collection of one or more items. A k-itemset is an itemset containing k items. The support count of an itemset is the number of transactions that contain it. Support is the support count divided by the total number of transactions. A frequent itemset is an itemset whose support is greater than or equal to a minimum-support threshold.

An association rule has the form X implies Y, where X and Y are itemsets. We evaluate a rule using support and confidence. Support measures how many transactions contain both X and Y. Confidence measures how often Y appears among the transactions that contain X. In practice, we keep only rules whose support is at least the minimum support threshold and whose confidence is at least the minimum confidence threshold. These thresholds are user-chosen hyperparameters of the mining process.

The brute-force approach is computationally prohibitive. If there are d unique items, there are two to the power of d possible itemsets, and the number of possible rules is enormous. Even with only a few items, the number of candidate rules grows quickly. We also observed that rules generated from the same frequent itemset have identical support, although their confidence values may differ.

Because of that, association rule mining is usually done in two steps. First, generate the frequent itemsets. Second, generate high-confidence rules from those frequent itemsets. The expensive part is still the frequent-itemset generation step.

In the brute-force version of frequent-itemset generation, every possible itemset is treated as a candidate, and we scan the database to count the support of each one. If N is the number of transactions, M is the number of candidate itemsets, and w is the transaction width, the cost is on the order of big O of N times M times w, which is expensive when M equals two to the power of d.

To reduce the search space, we use the Apriori principle. If an itemset is frequent, then all of its subsets must also be frequent. Equivalently, if an itemset is infrequent, then all of its supersets must also be infrequent. This follows from the anti-monotone property of support: support never increases when we add more items to a set.

The Apriori algorithm starts from frequent one-itemsets. Then it repeatedly generates candidate k plus one itemsets from frequent k-itemsets, prunes any candidate whose subsets are not all frequent, scans the database to count support, and keeps only the candidates that satisfy the minimum support threshold. This candidate-generation and pruning cycle continues until no new frequent itemsets are found.

One candidate-generation strategy is to merge frequent k minus one itemsets when their first k minus two items are identical. After generating those candidates, we prune any set that contains an infrequent subset before doing support counting.

We also discussed the cost of rule generation from a frequent itemset. If a frequent itemset L has k items, then there are two to the power of k, minus 2, non-empty proper subsets that can be used to form candidate rules. A brute-force approach is again expensive, so we usually rely on pruning based on confidence.

One question came up: what if two items are always sold together but are still not frequent overall? In that case, if their support is below the minimum support threshold, the frequent-itemset step removes them, so we will not discover their rule. That is a limitation of the minimum-support-based framework.

At the end, I briefly previewed hyperparameter tuning. Hyperparameters are values we choose before training, while parameters are learned during training. Different algorithms have different hyperparameters. For example, in K-means, the number of clusters is a hyperparameter, while the final centroids are learned parameters.

We mentioned several ways to tune hyperparameters: manual search, grid search, random search, and choosing values based on prior literature or common best practices. The purpose is to find settings that give strong model performance. In academic work, reviewers often ask why particular hyperparameter values were chosen, and one reasonable answer is that those values follow common practice in the literature. For example, batch sizes such as 32 or 64 are often used simply because they are widely adopted and empirically reasonable defaults.
