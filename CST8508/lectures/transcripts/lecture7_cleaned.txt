A model has three parts: the backbone, the neck, and the head. You can think of the neck as an extra model that takes the features coming out of the backbone, processes them, and passes them to the task heads that perform classification, object detection, or segmentation.

Student question: Can you not add the neck portion into the backbone like another layer?

Yes, you can. The reason I separate them is that when I talk about transfer learning, you can freeze the layers of your backbone but you might want to train your neck and your head. There are different ways to do transfer learning. You could initially freeze all of your backbone and only train the head, then slowly start unfreezing layers. The reason I've set it up like this is because OpenMMLab calls it backbone, neck, and head. You could consider the neck as part of the head itself, but it's good to conceptualize it this way because in a lot of the libraries, when you're taking a classification model, they call the last layer the head. As such, a neural network is just a bunch of layers, so you can put a layer as part of one or the other.

Student question: Is the neck limited in how many layers you can put, or can you just put as many as you want?

It was actually known as graduate student descent for the longest time, because we were just sitting and putting one layer, then another. You can think of neural networks as Lego blocks; each layer is a Lego block and you're just building. It's up to you how you build it, as long as it works.

What are the main steps in transfer learning? First, you need to define your model. You have a pre-trained model, and you take your own new model. That new model can be exactly the same as the pre-trained model, or it can be different and only initialize some parts with the pre-trained checkpoint. So first, you define your model architecture, and it's good to define it in terms of the backbone, the neck, and the head. Then you take a pre-trained checkpoint, take the weights from it, and load them into your model for each of the layers.

You can think of your saved model as a big dictionary, where the keys are the names of the layers. If you just print your model parameters in PyTorch, it will bring this whole thing up. You name your layers, for example conv1, batch_norm1, relu1. You can name them whatever you want, and with each layer you have all of these different parameters. When you load your checkpoint, you're loading this big dictionary into memory and then matching keys. You have your pre-trained checkpoint, and you've defined your model with layer 1, layer 2, and so on. You're taking the weights from the checkpoint and copying them onto your model. The keys need to match between your model definition and the checkpoint you're loading. If you find errors like "keys do not match," that means some layer keys don't match between the two.

Optionally, if you have less compute or want a staggered approach, you can freeze all the pre-trained layers, only train your head, and slowly start unfreezing. Or you can fine-tune all at once if you have a small dataset like the cats and dogs example. It depends on the data available, the compute available, and how much time you have. If you have only five minutes, you can just load the pre-trained checkpoint and run it as is.

Student question: How does this relate to continual learning?

Continual learning and restarting training are almost similar to transfer learning. Imagine you trained your network for 10 epochs and decide you should have trained it for 50. You restart your Colab session, load the saved checkpoint, and restart training. You're basically doing the same thing, but you don't have to worry about removing parameters because you're loading the same model again. When restarting training, you also need to load the optimizer settings. The optimizer settings include your learning rate, which is on a schedule, so you don't want to start with a high learning rate again at, say, iteration 420.

To summarize transfer learning: define your new model, load your checkpoint, copy the weights from the checkpoint into your model definition, and then optionally freeze some layers if you want, and train.

Before I go into how you do transfer learning, I want you to understand the ResNet model. When I talk about dimensions, it'll become clear, and based on the assignment, it seemed like many of you didn't really understand the architecture of ResNet. So this is a refresher.

Why is ResNet important? What does ResNet stand for? The big connection, also called the residual connection, is the main innovation in this model. When I read the paper, we were just like, "this is so dumb, just one extra connection and they get a paper at NeurIPS," which is the main conference for deep learning. But it actually worked, and so it kind of became standard.

When you were writing a config in MMPretrain, you put in a number of stages. Those stages are these things in the diagram. You can think of each of these blocks as a block of layers at the same spatial dimension.

Let's go through how this works. If you have an input image resized to 224 by 224 with 3 channels (RGB), it goes through your first convolution layer. The first convolution in ResNet-50 has a 7x7 filter with a stride of 2 and 64 filters. If somebody told you to deploy ResNet-50, you'd need to know the size of GPU you would need, which means knowing how many parameters this model has so it fits into your VRAM.

What does a 7x7 filter mean? You have 3 channels and a 7x7 filter, so each of the 64 filters is 7x7x3. You do the convolution: multiply the filter by the patch on each channel, then sum across channels to get one pixel value. That's the amount of compute that goes into producing each output pixel. That's why you do it on GPU, because they're all very parallelizable processes.

Do these dimensions make sense, how you get 112 out of 224 with a 7x7 filter and a stride of 2? You take your input, pad it, so the input size increases. Then the output is (input minus filter size) divided by stride, plus one.

That's the first layer. Then in max pooling, there are no learnable parameters; it's just taking the max. Generally max pooling is used to decrease the spatial size by 2, or by whatever the stride is. Here, 112 decreased by 2 gives you 56.

Then it goes into these blocks. Each block is a 1x1 convolution into a 3x3 convolution into a 1x1 convolution, plus a residual connection. Every time you look at a 1x1 convolution, think of it as a squeezing layer, because a 1x1 is not taking into account any context around your pixels; it's squashing all your channels.

This is an example of what a 1x1 convolution does. If this is your image and this is your filter, you multiply them, do the same for greens and blues, add them all up, and get one value. So 1x1 just combines the channels. It's a sum of all your channels, not an average; a sum. A 3x3 convolution takes the context into account. So in the ResNet block, what's happening is: it takes your input, squashes the channels, does the actual 3x3 convolution, and then expands it back up because you want to add it to your initial input via the skip connection and the dimensions need to match. That's why it's sandwiched between two 1x1 convolutions.

Student question: Why do you do the squeeze, then a 3x3?

What is the point of a convolutional neural network? Convolution detects features in your images. The more you stack convolution layers, initially it starts by detecting edges, then objects; the deeper you go, some filters detect eyes, some detect ears. They become specialized feature detectors on their own. Empirically, the deeper you go, the more advanced the feature detectors become.

Initially you start with a large filter receptive field to find global features like big edges. As you move deeper, you reduce the spatial resolution of feature maps to capture smaller features. So you start with a big spatial size and slowly decrease it. Empirically, they found that as you reduce the spatial resolution, you don't want to lose information. So they halve the spatial resolution but double the number of filters at each step, so your total information stays the same. Again, this isn't necessary; you could halve both. This is what that particular graduate student found, and they published a paper, so everybody used it. But you could halve both and maybe get no difference in your results.

Why the skip connection? It was the main innovation. As you keep increasing the layers, imagine a thousand layers. When you do backprop, you're computing gradients. You're computing dY/dX using the chain rule, so you multiply the gradient of this layer by the gradient of this layer. Now imagine multiplying 0.1 by 0.1 by 0.1; that's 10 to the minus 3. Over lots and lots of layers, you get a really, really small number. This is called the vanishing gradient problem, and it creates very unstable learning. So they said: pass the gradient backward through the block, but also give it a direct connection from the input. That way the gradient doesn't vanish as much.

Now the gradient path looks like you're directly skipping the block but also going through the block. So you're getting both the small gradient through the block and a direct gradient through the skip. This is repeated at every single stage. Each stage groups blocks at the same spatial dimension. By spatial dimension I mean 56x56, 28x28, etc. In one stage everything is 56x56, in the next everything is 28x28. As the spatial dimension halves, the number of filters doubles. That is just a choice for this model. The justification was that they're keeping the information the same, but you can make justifications after the fact. You'll find that everything in neural networks is very empirical; there isn't always a real reason behind it.

Do you understand why the 3x3 is sandwiched between two 1x1 convolutions? The first 1x1 is to squeeze. The second 1x1 expands it back up because you're adding it to the input through the skip connection, and you can't add tensors with different dimensions. So you want the dimensions to match.

Student question: When we squeeze, are we forcing the model to learn small, fine-grained details?

Yes. Usually in convolution we learn basic edges. The model also learns these style details.

Student question: When we're skipping, are we skipping in the backward pass or the forward pass?

Both. The model architecture has skip connections everywhere; there's a skip connection here, here, and here. This is just so you understand when I talk about cutting off the head and how many channels there are. You don't really need to understand this in so much detail, but it's good to know because ResNet is one of the main models used in computer vision.

Student question: What's the goal after the squeeze?

The goal is to run the 3x3 over a smaller, squeezed version. The squeezing concentrates information so that the 3x3, which is the main convolution that finds new features, operates on more information in less space. It's also easier for computation.

The whole goal is to show that conceptually each library does the same thing through different routes. For PyTorch, the pre-trained library is called torchvision.models. Because it's so common to do transfer learning for classification, many libraries have a shortcut for removing the head for classification. For PyTorch and Hugging Face, you set requires_grad to False and it's done by name. Keras handles this similarly, but it's not as trivial there.

To freeze the model, whichever parameters you want to freeze, you set requires_grad to False. Hugging Face and PyTorch are similar in this aspect. Keras gives you a shortcut: base_model.trainable = False freezes all the previously trainable layers. In OpenMM, you set frozen_stages.

Training loops: for PyTorch you write your own manual training loop. For Hugging Face, you have the Trainer API. For Keras, you have model.fit(). For OpenMMLab, you have runner.train(). Since OpenMMLab is based on PyTorch, there are different ways of doing it.

For deployment: it's much easier to deploy models on Hugging Face; you just push to the Hugging Face Hub. Keras has model files that Google uses. OpenMMLab has a separate library for deployment called MMDeploy.

As I mentioned, use PyTorch for custom code and research, for things not defined in the libraries. Hugging Face is easy for fast iteration. For TensorFlow, most people have dropped support, so I'd only use it for use cases requiring really fast serving, for mobile or edge devices or small models. TensorFlow shines in those cases because of TFLite. OpenMM is a computer vision specific framework.

The core of object detection is not only to tell you what's in the image, but where it is. If your input is an image with a cat, dog, bicycle, and a bucket, the output for the cat will be the label "cat" and a bounding box around it, giving you XY coordinates. The main point of object detection is what is in the image and where it is. In classification, you're only saying what is in the image. So if you had to take a classification model and convert it to object detection, how would you do that? That's the question I'm trying to answer in these next few slides.

Say I had the same ResNet-50 backbone. For classification, I add an average pooling layer, flatten it, and add a softmax for the 1000 classes I'm classifying. For object detection, I want to say what's in the image and the bounding box, so I want some non-trivial processing from my feature extractor that gives me both the label of the bounding box and the coordinates of the bounding box. I need a neck that does non-trivial processing that can give you both label and coordinates. That's why we have the neck.

There are mainly 3 ways of doing object detection: YOLO, SSD (Single Shot Detector), and Feature Pyramid Network. I actually don't know what YOLO stands for. We're now on YOLO version 12 or so. I don't know if the name was meant as a meme or if it actually means something. I'll talk through why each was initially developed.

Initially people developed YOLO. The thesis: your convolution network, as you go through the layers, has learned nice feature detectors. So you have these detectors that can tell you where particular things are and what they are. YOLO uses the very last feature map you get, the 7x7 final layer, and feeds that to your object detection algorithm. Think of object detection as just another neural network that takes in features and tells you the XY coordinates of the bounding box for that particular thing.

In this case, you use the last feature map. If you were making a ResNet-based YOLO model, you take the whole network, get the output from the last feature map, and put it into your neck. You define a new neck that's an object detector; you can inherit from any object detector in the library and add a new classification head. You have a classification head, and your object detector module that gives you bounding boxes. The classification gives you what's in each bounding box, and you get labels for your image.

What was the problem with YOLO? The problem was using just one feature map for all of the objects in the image. The CNN starts wide and goes narrow, narrow, narrow. The final feature map is 7x7, so each cell has a large receptive field; it captures big features but misses tiny far-away objects. If you have a tiny far-away dog or far-away person, that would be totally missed by YOLO. Version 1 of YOLO totally missed those; it detected everything from one feature map but missed all the tiny objects.

People wanted to capture objects at different scales. So they came up with SSD (Single Shot Detector). Instead of using only the final layer, you take the output from each stage and feed it into your object detector. You get feature maps at different resolutions, so you can capture both big and small objects.

But what was the problem there? If I take from the earlier layers, those have learned only rudimentary features; they have good spatial resolution but only see basic features like edges. So you don't get the complex context around your objects.

People wanted both: the rich semantic context of the final layer, but also a big filter and a small filter. So you want both resolution and broad context information. They came up with the Feature Pyramid Network, which is the standard for object detection right now. A lot of the Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN models use this Feature Pyramid Network. This will be your object detection model, part of your neck.

What FPN does, for each layer, is take the rudimentary feature map and the final feature map and add them together. You get both the semantic detail from the last layer and the spatial resolution of the early layers. Your next lecture might cover only YOLO and SSD; I don't know if it covers Feature Pyramid Network.

The main goal is to get objects at every resolution but with as much context information as the last layer. It has non-trivial processing in your neck. The whole point is to show you that your neck can be extremely complex. In theory, you can take a classification model and convert it to an object detection model, but it's slightly non-trivial; you have to add this complexity in the neck.
