Now let's compare detection models and classification models. The outputs of these two models differ. The classification model's output is different from the detection model's output. Now the two types of detection heads. We have the input passing through the backbone, neck, and detection head, where the decision is made. This detection head can be of different types. One is anchor-based. Anchor-based means you provide some predefined bounding boxes. When you train the model, you know exactly where the bounding box should be; that is the ground truth. You know the true value of that object. If you have an image, you know the coordinates. This is called the ground truth value, the actual coordinate. In the anchor-based approach, we give some predefined boxes at different scales and coordinates. These predefined boxes are adjusted to match with the ground truth. This is how the training is done. After training, we want to adjust the predicted values to best match the ground truth. That is the anchor-based technique. In anchor-based detection, we use predefined bounding boxes of various sizes and aspect ratios. These predefined bounding boxes are adjusted during training to match the ground truth. An example is Faster R-CNN. Faster R-CNN generates region proposals based on these anchors. The model knows the object is in some area near the anchor and adjusts the values to fit the object. In the example image, you can see the ground truth boxes, which are the actual locations of the bounding boxes. There is one around the cat and one around the dog; these are the ground truth. We give some predefined boxes of different sizes that closely match the objects. So we use different sizes or aspect ratios of predefined boxes. These anchors are adjusted during training to match with the ground truth. This is what the anchor-based approach looks like: you give predefined boxes.

Another type is anchorless. Here, we don't have any anchors or predefined bounding boxes given during training. Instead, we directly predict the corners or centers of objects. We see whether we can actually predict the corner or the center. You're not creating a bounding box around the object; instead, you predict just the center or the corner. Examples include CornerNet and CenterNet, whose architectures you can browse. Those are examples of anchorless detection heads. These directly predict the centers of the objects. The advantage is that they are not as complex as the anchor-based approach, because anchor-based requires different aspect ratios of predefined boxes and you need to adjust those boxes, which is an additional task. Here, you only predict the center or corner of the object. This is computationally less complex, but the problem is the accuracy is not as great as the anchor-based methods. So anchor-based methods give better accuracy than anchorless ones.
