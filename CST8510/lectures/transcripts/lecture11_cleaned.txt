You can use a simple way: take the majority answer and say that is the output.

Now you can do this slightly better. You can go one step further. This only gives the correct answer roughly one-third to 60 percent of the time. Only if the top answer, the one that occurred the maximum number of times, occurred more than the threshold. That threshold you can decide depending upon your comfort with risk.

For example, you can say that if the proportion of the output is more than 0.5, then I will give that answer. If all the answer proportions are below 0.5, then it is an uncertain answer. Then you should say, "I do not know." So if the unique output proportion is less than 0.5, say, "I do not know the answer." Then you ask for support, or you give a friendly answer of "I do not know." If the top frequent answer's proportion is more than the threshold, you say the answer is that.

This is called uncertainty thresholding with self-consistency. You are doing more than self-consistency here. You are putting a threshold on what level of consistency you need. Clear?

Then you can improve this again. How? You take these uncertain answers, the ones which gave less than 0.5 proportions, or the top ones. For those scenarios, take the questions which have a lot of answers below your threshold. Give the correct answer and put it in your prompt, your chain of thought, as few-shot learning examples. If you are anyway teaching the LLM on questions where it is struggling, you keep doing this. If it is answering a lot of questions whose confidence level is low, then take those, add the correct answer, and add them to the pool of few-shot learning examples. Now we are talking about RAG. In simple RAG, you take a set of documents, break them into chunks, convert each chunk into an embedding vector, and store it in the vector store. Then at inference time, take the query, convert it to an embedding, query against this vector store, retrieve the context, combine the context with the original query, send it to the LLM, and get the answer. All of you know this, right?

But this is very basic and doesn't always give satisfactory answers. There are a lot of problems, because the main issue is in the way you vectorize your data. It is not necessary that your answer precisely lies in one chunk. You would have to keep some overlapping chunks. And it is not always the case that the query and answers are semantically close, because a lot of answers may be similar. So simple RAG may not be good enough to get you very good recall and precision in your response.
